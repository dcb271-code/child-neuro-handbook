ANSWER KEY AND EXPLANATIONS

1. E. Risk after a single bite is very small; most infected patients develop erythema migrans even without a remembered bite. (Current IDSA guidance permits single-dose doxycycline when the tick is an engorged Ixodes attached over 36 hr in a highly endemic area.)

2. A. Fumarylacetoacetate hydrolase deficiency; succinylacetone accumulation causes porphyria-like neurologic crises and renal tubulopathy; HCC in about 40% untreated. Nitisinone (NTBC) plus diet has transformed prognosis. Cataracts are galactosemia, not tyrosinemia.

3. C. Associated with GAS, TB, Yersinia, histoplasmosis, coccidioidomycosis, IBD, SLE, JRA, sarcoidosis, sulfonamides, phenytoin, OCPs.

4. E. Impacted stool (meconium ileus equivalent) from insufficient pancreatic enzyme replacement; consider fibrosing colonopathy if on high-dose enzymes.

5. A. Risk is highest under age 2, with polytherapy, and with underlying metabolic disease (POLG mutations are a contraindication). Pancreatitis is the other idiosyncratic toxicity.

6. D. Clinical improvement within 6-24 hr is the best predictor; no lab or imaging test has predictive power. Management is supportive: normal oxygenation and blood pressure, no hyperventilation, no dextrose loading.

7. D. Neonatal lupus: transplacental IgG (anti-Ro/La) causes rash (often UV-provoked), cytopenias, hepatitis, and heart block; all resolve except the block. Only 1 in 10 has both rash and ECG changes, so serology is the diagnostic step; ECG is also reasonable.

8. C. Y material is present in 5-10%; gonadoblastoma risk warrants prophylactic gonadectomy.

9. E. Hydrocephalus, from the associated Chiari II malformation, develops in most; head ultrasound or CT at birth and serial head circumference.

10. B. Ash-leaf hypomelanotic macules are tuberous sclerosis (present in over 90%). Lisch nodules are present in the vast majority of NF1 patients after age 12. (Revised 2021 criteria add heterozygous pathogenic NF1 variant and choroidal abnormalities.)

11. D. Right-to-left shunt bypasses pulmonary filtration; other predisposing conditions are chronic otitis and sinusitis, endocarditis, and immunodeficiency. Ring-enhancing lesion on imaging; LP is contraindicated.

12. D. Bulbar palsy (weak suck, feeble cry, drooling, ptosis, sluggish pupils) precedes descending symmetric weakness; constipation is early but soft stools do not exclude it; reflexes are preserved early. Most cases have no honey exposure (environmental spores). Confirm by toxin or organism in stool; EMG shows brief small-amplitude potentials with incremental response to rapid repetitive stimulation (presynaptic).

13. C. Sensorineural hearing loss, potentially attenuated by dexamethasone (best evidence for Hib); audiology before discharge. Seizures occur in 20-50% acutely (cytokines, fever) but chronic epilepsy is unusual.

14. D. Adherent membrane extending beyond the faucial area, relatively little fever, and dysphagia distinguish diphtheria from streptococcal and EBV pharyngitis.

15. C. Folate must be replete for a pyridoxine trial to be interpretable; about half of patients are B6-responsive. Nonresponders need methionine restriction and betaine.

16. A. Abdominal pain, vasculitic rash, arthritis, nephritis with normal platelets; GI involvement may progress to intussusception. A preoperative UA showing hematuria and proteinuria in a child labeled "acute abdomen" should cancel the operation pending evaluation. Coronary aneurysm is not an HSP complication (Kawasaki).

17. A. Bacterial tracheitis, most often S. aureus (current practice adds MRSA coverage).

18. B. EEG stratifies recurrence risk and identifies a syndrome; treatment is not started after a single unprovoked seizure with normal exam. MRI is reserved for focal features, abnormal exam, or focal EEG.

19. C. Response to social stimuli is a critical observation; this 3-mo-old had pneumococcal meningitis.

20. E. Hypotrichosis is the rule. Inability to sweat causes recurrent high fevers, so it belongs in the FUO differential.

21. D. NF1 is caused by heterogeneous loss-of-function variants (about half de novo). Repeat disorders show anticipation; Friedreich ataxia (GAA intronic, recessive) is the exception to dominant inheritance.

22. C. The neural tube closes by day 28, before most pregnancies are recognized. 0.4 mg daily for all women of childbearing potential; 4 mg for a prior affected pregnancy, which also lowers recurrence.

23. E. Hypomelanotic macules (ash-leaf or confetti) are the earliest sign; use a Wood lamp. TSC1/TSC2, mTOR pathway; cortical tubers, subependymal nodules, SEGA, cardiac rhabdomyomas, renal angiomyolipomas; vigabatrin for infantile spasms, mTOR inhibitors for SEGA.

24. E. Poststreptococcal OCD and chorea (Sydenham spectrum; the PANDAS construct remains contested).

25. A. Kawasaki is uncommon after age 5, less severe, and not associated with hypotension or diarrhea.

26. E. HMBS deficiency, autosomal dominant with low penetrance; rare before puberty; precipitated by drugs (barbiturates, sulfonamides), fasting, stress, and menses. No photosensitivity. Urine PBG during the attack; treat with hemin and glucose.

27. B. Reactive arthritis needs only symptomatic NSAID therapy. Triggers: Shigella, Salmonella, Yersinia, Campylobacter, Cryptosporidium, Giardia, Chlamydia.

28. A. Symptomatic parapneumonic effusion; both diagnostic and therapeutic.

29. E. More common in boys. Acquired epileptic aphasia (verbal auditory agnosia) with sleep-activated spikes (often electrical status in slow sleep); valproate, steroids, or benzodiazepines; GRIN2A in a subset.

30. C. Symptomatic hemo/pneumothorax with shock is evacuated before imaging.

31. A. Beard-distribution hemangioma may herald a subglottic hemangioma that can obstruct the airway. (Propranolol is now standard therapy.)

32. E. Absence of male-to-male transmission with all daughters affected is the signature. Examples: X-linked hypophosphatemic rickets, incontinentia pigmenti (male-lethal), Rett syndrome (MECP2, mostly de novo).

33. B. Sacral hair tufts, hemangiomas, dimples above the gluteal cleft, and lipomas mark occult dysraphism (tethered cord). Ultrasound suffices before ossification of the posterior elements (under about 3 mo); MRI thereafter.

34. B. Somatic activating GNAQ mutation; ipsilateral parieto-occipital leptomeningeal angioma with tram-track calcification, contralateral hemiparesis, and glaucoma. Aspirin and early seizure control are used to limit stroke-like episodes.

35. C. Hypotonia is the rule. A major Jones criterion; latency of months after streptococcal pharyngitis so ASO may be normal; penicillin prophylaxis and echocardiography.

36. B. B19 infects erythroid precursors, producing fetal anemia and nonimmune hydrops; intrauterine transfusion can be curative.

37. C. Biotinidase (or holocarboxylase synthetase) deficiency impairs the four biotin-dependent carboxylases; ketoacidosis, hyperammonemia, seizures, hearing loss, optic atrophy, dermatitis, and alopecia. Oral biotin is curative; on newborn screen.

38. B. Outbreaks occur but person-to-person transmission is uncommon.

39. B. A persistent or worsening focal consolidation after apparent recovery warrants bronchoscopy.

40. E. Awareness is retained; some patients converse during the event.

41. D. Completed primary series plus a contaminated wound with more than 5 yr since last booster. (Tdap is now preferred for the adolescent booster.)

42. D. Reducing substances can be negative if lactose intake has been curtailed; enzyme assay is definitive (invalid after transfusion). Classic galactosemia predisposes to E. coli sepsis and cataracts; on newborn screen.

43. B. Insidious afebrile staccato cough with crackles and without wheeze distinguishes it from RSV; treat with oral erythromycin 14 days (topical therapy of the conjunctivitis does not prevent pneumonia).

44. C. Canavan disease (aspartoacylase, ASPA; autosomal recessive, Ashkenazi founder alleles); NAA elevated in urine, blood, and on MR spectroscopy. Macrocephalic leukodystrophy with optic atrophy; Alexander disease is the other macrocephalic leukodystrophy (frontal predominance, GFAP).

45. D. Disseminated neonatal HSV: hepatitis, coagulopathy, and shock with negative bacterial cultures. Localized SEM and disseminated disease present at a mean of 5-6 days; CNS disease at 8-12 days.

46. A. Pompe disease (GSD II, lysosomal acid maltase); the short PR with massive LVH distinguishes it from SMA. Vacuolar myopathy on biopsy; enzyme assay on dried blood spot is now the first-line test, and alglucosidase alfa ERT changed a uniformly fatal infantile course. On newborn screen in many states.

47. D. Macrolide prophylaxis for all household and close contacts regardless of age, immunization status, or symptoms. (Azithromycin is now preferred, especially in young infants.)

48. A. Facial palsy is relatively common in pediatric Lyme disease and may be the only manifestation; it usually resolves in 2-8 wk whether or not treated. Serology is unnecessary with erythema migrans; LP is reserved for meningeal signs.

49. C. US and European cohorts; higher in Africa and Haiti. Prenatal, intrapartum, and 6-wk neonatal zidovudine cut transmission to 3-4% (now under 1% with suppressive maternal ART).

50. B. Antimycobacterial therapy only if M. tuberculosis cannot be excluded or chronic drainage develops. Lymphadenitis is the most common NTM disease in children.

ANSWER KEY AND EXPLANATIONS

1. B. IgM-VCA is the best single test for acute infection; anti-EBNA appears 3-4 mo after infection and its presence argues against acute disease.

2. D. VLCAD (originally described as LCAD) involves skeletal and cardiac muscle; SCAD does not produce hypoketotic hypoglycemia.

3. B. A positive ANA is a nonspecific marker of increased lymphocyte activity in many inflammatory states.

4. D. Minimum normal systolic for age 1-10 is 70 + 2 x age, here 88. Isotonic crystalloid without dextrose.

5. C. Risk of later epilepsy rises with family history of epilepsy, preexisting neurodevelopmental abnormality, and complex features (focal, prolonged, recurrent within 24 hr). About 30% recur with future fevers; that recurrence is predicted by young age, low-grade fever, brief fever-to-seizure interval, and family history of febrile seizures.

6. C. (source key). Epiglottitis in the unimmunized: high fever, toxicity, air hunger, drooling, without barking cough. (Drooling is the more commonly taught discriminator; the source keys the cough.)

7. B. No agreed indication for systemic steroids; management is burn-unit style supportive care, withdrawal of the culprit drug, and topical ophthalmic steroids to limit scarring. Secondary bacterial infection and sepsis are the main threats; antibiotics are for proven or strongly suspected infection.

8. E. Autosomal recessive HEXA deficiency; the common Ashkenazi allele is a 4-bp insertion (frameshift), the French Canadian allele a large deletion. Late-onset and juvenile forms exist as well.

9. B. Single large-scale mtDNA deletion (usually sporadic); onset before 20, ragged red fibers, elevated CSF protein, cerebellar ataxia, hearing loss, endocrinopathy. Conduction block is the lethal complication and warrants a pacemaker.

10. E. Lower motor neuron signs at the cervical level with upper motor neuron signs below localize to the cervical cord; bladder involvement and progression demand urgent MRI.

11. D. Early handedness is pathologic. Perinatal arterial stroke is often multifactorial, but prothrombotic evaluation (factor V Leiden, prothrombin G20210A, protein C and S, antithrombin, antiphospholipid antibodies, lipoprotein(a)) is standard.

12. B. Guillain-Barre: albuminocytologic dissociation, areflexia, symmetric ascending weakness, prolonged F waves early; IVIG. Campylobacter (also CMV, EBV, Mycoplasma) is the classic antecedent; ganglioside molecular mimicry.

13. A. Solitary cyst with or without enhancement, or a scolex; the leading cause of acquired epilepsy worldwide. Albendazole plus steroids for viable parenchymal cysts; calcified lesions need only seizure control.

14. E. Retinol concentration correlates with severity; vitamin A reduces morbidity and mortality and is recommended for severe measles in the US as well.

15. C. RCDP (PEX7 or plasmalogen synthesis defects) shows elevated phytanic acid and low erythrocyte plasmalogens with normal VLCFA. Zellweger, NALD, and infantile Refsum form the PEX-gene biogenesis spectrum.

16. D. Pulmonary-renal vasculitis that responds to therapy.

17. E. Narcotics are not triggers; succinylcholine and volatile agents are. Succinylcholine also risks hyperkalemia, myoglobinuria, and raised ICP.

18. E. Risk is amplified by rapid titration and co-administration of valproate (inhibits lamotrigine glucuronidation). Phenobarbital and carbamazepine cause hypersensitivity syndrome with hepatitis; HLA-B*15:02 predicts carbamazepine SJS.

19. B. Congenital diaphragmatic hernia; decompress bowel occupying the chest and avoid bag-mask ventilation.

20. A. Lindane is an alternative but potentially neurotoxic; treat household contacts.

21. D. CGG repeat expansion in FMR1 over 200 (normal 6-54; premutation 55-200) with promoter methylation; the most common inherited cause of intellectual disability. Premutation carriers are at risk for FXTAS and premature ovarian insufficiency.

22. C. X-linked dominant, almost exclusively in girls (and XXY males); chorioretinal lacunae are pathognomonic; poor prognosis.

23. E. Retrobulbar optic neuritis with dissemination in time and space suggests multiple sclerosis; MRI shows periventricular and spinal plaques. In children, MOG antibody disease and NMOSD (aquaporin-4) must be distinguished.

24. B. A ciliopathy (dozens of genes); hypotonia, ataxia, episodic hyperpnea and apnea, oculomotor apraxia, and variable renal, retinal, and hepatic involvement.

25. A. Duchenne (X-linked; about two thirds deletions or duplications, the rest point variants); in-frame deletions produce the milder Becker phenotype (reading-frame rule). CK is markedly elevated; steroids, cardiac surveillance, exon-skipping and gene therapies.

26. B. Periventricular calcifications are characteristic of congenital CMV; toxoplasmosis produces diffuse parenchymal calcifications.

27. B. Cystathionine beta-synthase deficiency, autosomal recessive; downward lens dislocation (upward in Marfan), thromboembolism, osteoporosis, scoliosis, seizures; cardiomyopathy is not a feature. Short limbs are not part of the phenotype.

28. C. 29. A. Signs of poor CNS perfusion; crystalloid immediately, then rapid antibiotics; steroids only if adrenal insufficiency demonstrated. Multiple organ system failure is the factor that most clearly predicts mortality in shock.

50. A 15-yr-old on IV ampicillin for pneumonia develops bloody diarrhea on hospital day 6; she is otherwise improved. Most appropriate action?
- A) Discontinue ampicillin
- B) V/Q scan
- C) Abdominal CT
- D) Switch to a third-generation cephalosporin
- E) Add erythromycin or doxycycline


ANSWER KEY AND EXPLANATIONS

1. E. Salmonella is by far the most common cause of febrile gastroenteritis in early infancy; Campylobacter is second. Shigella is rare in infancy and common at 1-3 yr; rotavirus rarely causes bloody stool.

2. D. Organic acidemia: ketoacidosis with anion gap, hyperammonemia, neutropenia. Some MMA is cobalamin-responsive (1-2 mg/day hydroxocobalamin); carnitine and protein restriction otherwise. Propionic acidemia is the mimic. On newborn screen (C3 acylcarnitine).

3. B. CK was 7,500 IU/mL. JDM classically affects preadolescent girls with insidious proximal weakness and vasculitic rash over knuckles, malar area, or eyelids. Dysphagia and drooling later reflect palatopharyngeal striated muscle weakness.

4. D. Persistent hypoxia on 100% FiO2 is respiratory failure.

5. C. Onset 1-3 mo after starting aromatic anticonvulsants; hepatitis and lymphadenopathy are uncommon in SJS or TEN. Cross-reactivity among phenytoin, carbamazepine, and phenobarbital. (HLA-B*15:02 screening in Asian populations for carbamazepine.)

6. D. 4-6 Hz polyspike-wave, often photosensitive; valproate or levetiracetam; lifelong treatment is usual and carbamazepine can aggravate.

7. B. Repeated early crystalloid boluses; transfuse as blood loss becomes evident. Abdominal CT with IV contrast is the standard study in stable blunt trauma.

8. A. NF1-associated optic pathway gliomas (pilocytic, about 15% of patients) are usually indolent; treatment (carboplatin-vincristine, or selumetinib) is reserved for visual decline or progression, and radiation is avoided because of secondary malignancy and moyamoya risk.

9. E. A karyotype distinguishes nondisjunction (recurrence about 1%) from a translocation; if translocation, both parents are studied, and a carrier parent raises sibling recurrence to about 10-15% (higher for maternal carriers, 100% for a 21;21 carrier).

10. E. Congenital CMV causes microcephaly. Marrow expansion, storage disease and leukodystrophy (Canavan, Alexander, Tay-Sachs, GA1), CSF excess, subdural collections, and benign familial macrocephaly enlarge the head.

11. B. Homozygous SMN1 deletion (about 95%); SMN2 copy number modifies severity. Alert face, bell-shaped chest, paradoxical breathing. Nusinersen, risdiplam, and onasemnogene make newborn screening and presymptomatic treatment the standard.

12. C. Posterior fossa tumor with raised ICP; papilledema takes time to develop and must be sought in every child with headache. Acutely, visual acuity is preserved in papilledema, unlike papillitis of optic neuritis.

13. E. Summer and fall; CSF PCR has replaced culture. Late-summer encephalitis after freshwater or saltwater marsh exposure in the Northeast raises eastern equine encephalitis; HSV is the treatable cause not to miss.

14. D. Doxycycline is preferred at any age; chloramphenicol carries higher mortality, and dental staining is unlikely with a single doxycycline course.

15. A. Branched-chain ketoacid dehydrogenase deficiency; leucine is the neurotoxic amino acid. Crisis management is dialysis or hemofiltration plus reversal of catabolism; on newborn screen.

16. C. Complement is normal in nephrotic syndrome; immune complex diseases reduce CH50.

17. C. Chemical burn or pressure necrosis; immediate removal, avoiding pushing it posteriorly.

18. D. Postzygotic activating GNAS mutation; irregular "coast of Maine" borders contrast with the smooth "coast of California" macules of NF1; hyperfunctioning endocrinopathies.

19. A. Syncope produces loss of tone and rapid recovery once supine, though a few convulsive jerks may occur; a prolonged postictal state points to seizure.

20. B. Fencing that cannot be climbed; children under 3-4 cannot swim safely; covers are dangerous.

21. D. P is posterior fossa (Dandy-Walker, cerebellar hypoplasia). Cerebrovascular anomalies are the major source of morbidity and shape the safety of propranolol.

22. A. Roughly twice the background risk for an autosomal recessive trait in nonconsanguineous matings (first cousins share 1/8 of their genes; coefficient of inbreeding 1/16).

23. C. Self-limited once imaging excludes a chiasmatic or hypothalamic glioma.

24. D. CMT1A, 17p12 duplication, autosomal dominant; onion bulbs on nerve biopsy. Deletion of the same segment causes hereditary neuropathy with liability to pressure palsies.

25. E. Only before substantial cerebral involvement (low Loes score, minimal symptoms), which is the rationale for newborn screening and serial MRI in presymptomatic boys. Lorenzo's oil normalizes VLCFA but does not arrest cerebral disease.

26. B. Primary amebic meningoencephalitis via the cribriform plate; purulent CSF with motile trophozoites on wet mount; nearly always fatal despite amphotericin and miltefosine.

27. D. Indications: positive prenatal screen, GBS bacteriuria in pregnancy, or a previous infant with invasive GBS disease; unknown status with risk factors (preterm, ROM 18 hr or more, intrapartum fever). Prelabor cesarean with intact membranes does not require prophylaxis.

28. B. Phenylalanine hydroxylase deficiency; the odor is phenylacetate. Children appear normal at birth and are identified by newborn screening; overtreatment causes anorexia, lethargy, rash, and diarrhea from phenylalanine deficiency.

29. B. Before aneurysms form, severe myocarditis can cause cardiogenic shock. Splenic rupture is not a feature; arthritis, aseptic meningitis, urethritis, and gallbladder hydrops are.

30. D. Worse with URIs and supine; self-limited.

31. C. Benign, self-limited, in about 50% of term infants; peaks day 2; spares palms and soles. Transient neonatal pustular melanosis shows neutrophils and leaves hyperpigmented macules.

32. B. Provoked by hyperventilation; ethosuximide first-line. Slow spike-wave is Lennox-Gastaut. Hyperventilation-induced 3 Hz slowing without spikes is a normal finding in children.

33. E. Otitis and viral or bacterial (S. aureus, pneumococcus) pneumonia are common; benign acute childhood myositis (calf pain, elevated CK) follows influenza B. Reye syndrome follows salicylate use during influenza or varicella, which is why aspirin is avoided in febrile children.

34. C. GCDH deficiency; striatal necrosis during catabolic crisis leaves a dystonic-dyskinetic movement disorder; subdural hematomas and retinal hemorrhages can mimic abusive head trauma (as can Menkes disease and osteogenesis imperfecta). On newborn screen (C5DC); lysine-restricted diet, carnitine, and aggressive sick-day management prevent crises.

35. A. Shiga toxin-producing E. coli O157:H7 causes most HUS; S. dysenteriae serotype 1 is occasionally responsible.

36. E. Persistent hyperinsulinism (formerly nesidioblastosis); K-ATP channel defects (ABCC8, KCNJ11) are the most common, familial or sporadic. Diazoxide or octreotide; some need near-total pancreatectomy (diffuse) or focal resection. Symptomatic neonatal hypoglycemia gets a 2 mL/kg D10W minibolus followed by infusion.

37. C. Diagnosis requires 4 of 11 criteria (1997 ACR; since revised by SLICC 2012 and EULAR/ACR 2019).

38. D. Postnatal exposure also matters, but prenatal maternal smoking dominates. Prematurity, low birth weight, prone position, and African-American heritage are associated; immunizations are not. Long QT and MCAD are rare associations to suspect when SIDS recurs in a family.

39. E. Not a complication of ordinary infantile hemangioma.

40. B. About 70% in sleep; hemifacial sensorimotor seizures with drooling and speech arrest; centrotemporal spikes activated by sleep; remits by adolescence.

41. A. Yield about 50%; bronchoscopy is lower. Negative cultures never exclude TB, and the child is treated on epidemiologic grounds.

42. C. X-linked; most common urea cycle defect. Orotic aciduria distinguishes OTC from CPS1 and NAGS deficiency (low orotate); respiratory alkalosis without acidosis distinguishes urea cycle defects from organic acidemias. Heterozygous females can present later with Reye-like episodes.

43. A. Reactive skin test without clinical or radiographic disease defines infection; 5-10% of untreated infected persons progress to disease. Treat with isoniazid 9 mo (or current rifampin-based short regimens).

44. E. Glucose-6-phosphatase deficiency (von Gierke): fasting hypoglycemia with lactic acidosis, hyperuricemia, hyperlipidemia, and doll facies; no glycemic response to glucagon. Oral fructose (and galactose) will not raise glucose because hepatic release of free glucose requires the deficient enzyme; uncooked cornstarch does.

45. B. Healthy dogs, cats, and ferrets are held for 10-day observation; prophylaxis begins at the first sign of rabies in the animal, which is then euthanized and tested.

46. A. ABCD1 defect; parieto-occipital demyelination with contrast-enhancing edge, central auditory processing loss, adrenal insufficiency (hyperpigmentation; check ACTH stimulation). VLCFA may be normal in some heterozygous females, so carrier testing requires mutation analysis. HSCT before symptoms (Loes score) or elivaldogene gene therapy halts cerebral disease; on newborn screen in many states.

47. B. Nontreponemal tests (VDRL, RPR) are quantifiable and usually revert within 1 yr of adequate treatment of primary syphilis; treponemal tests (MHA-TP, FTA-ABS) usually stay positive for life.

48. A. Aldolase B deficiency; asymptomatic until sucrose or fructose exposure; older children learn to avoid sweets. Fructose and sucrose elimination is curative.

49. C. Snuffles is congenital syphilis.

50. A. The first step in C. difficile-associated diarrhea is stopping the inciting antibiotic; toxins A and B mediate disease. (Current: oral vancomycin or fidaxomicin for confirmed disease.)
